---
title: Working with Matchboard
description: Why Matchboard is more useful when you use it throughout the coaching cycle, not only afterwards.
---

Matchboard can be used as a record keeper: you can enter a final score and a list of who
played after every match, and Matchboard will hold that record. That works, but it is not
where Matchboard is most useful.

Matchboard gets more useful the more of the coaching cycle it sees:

```text
plan -> prepare -> run the match -> reflect -> learn -> plan again
```

Each stage adds something the next stage can use. Recording only the final result tells you
what happened, but not what was intended, what actually changed during the match, or what the
coach learned from it. Use Matchboard while you coach, not only after you coach, and each
match becomes a fuller, more comparable piece of history rather than an isolated scoreline.

## What each stage adds

### Planning adds intent

When you set up [selections, lineups, and rotations](/docs/squad-planning) before a match, you
are telling Matchboard what you meant to do — who was meant to start, in what position, and
what role players like a [support](/docs/squad-planning) or
[development](/docs/rotations) movement were meant to play. A final score alone cannot tell
anyone that later.

### Matchday use adds reality

Recording [who actually played, for how long, and in which positions](/docs/matchday) during
the match tells Matchboard what really happened. The plan and the match itself can differ —
recording both makes that difference visible instead of losing it.

### Post-match work adds interpretation

Completing the [post-match report and observations](/docs/post-match) adds the coach's read on
how the match went while it is still fresh. Raw events — a goal, a substitution, a final score
— do not explain why the match unfolded the way it did. The coach's observation does.

### Repeated use adds history

Using this workflow consistently, round after round, builds a comparable history across
players, opponents, and team compositions. See [Evidence and learning](/docs/evidence-and-learning)
for what that history looks like once several matches have contributed to it. One match is a
single data point; a season of matches recorded the same way is a basis for better-informed
planning.

## Partial use still has value

None of this is all-or-nothing. If you only ever enter results after the match, Matchboard
still stores a useful record — but it has less context, because it never captured what was
planned or what the coach noticed at the time. A round with only a final score is less useful
to look back on than a round with a plan, an actual account of what happened, and a coach's
observation attached to it.

The goal is not to complete every field for its own sake. It is to record the parts of the
coaching cycle that actually happened around a match, because each one adds context that later
views and evidence can draw on.

## What this looks like in practice

Fjordvik FK's coach prepares Fjordvik Rød for a match against Bergstad IF. Elias Storm is given
an intended starting position and a planned second-half rotation. During the match, the
rotation happens earlier than planned — the coach records the actual position change and
minutes as they occur. After the match, the coach adds a short observation about how the change
worked out. Later, when reviewing Elias Storm's history or preparing for the next match against
Bergstad IF, that complete account — plan, reality, and interpretation — is what makes the
history genuinely useful, rather than a single remembered scoreline.

## What Matchboard does not claim

Evidence built up this way is decision support, not an objective verdict on a player or a
match. A handful of matches do not prove anything on their own — they add context that a coach
can weigh alongside everything else they already know about a player. See
[Evidence and learning](/docs/evidence-and-learning) for what Matchboard does and does not
claim to know from recorded matches.
