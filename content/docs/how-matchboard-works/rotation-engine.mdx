description: How Matchboard plans a whole match's substitutions and position changes, and why the result is reasonable rather than claimed optimal.

## What

A rotation plan is a sequence of changes across a whole match — not one isolated substitution
decided in the moment, but a full picture of who is on the pitch at every stage, built before
kickoff from the starting lineup onward.

## Starting from the lineup, not from scratch

The plan's first state is the match's own starting lineup — see
[Lineup engine](/docs/how-matchboard-works/lineup-engine). Every later state in the plan is
computed from the state before it, not independently: a position swap at one point in the plan
changes what "who is on the pitch" means for every decision after it.

```text
Starting lineup
      │
      ▼
Planned change 1  (who is on the pitch after this is the input to the next step)
      │
      ▼
Planned change 2
      │
      ▼
        ...through to full time
```

## Where changes can happen

Matchboard does not consider every second of the match as a possible change point. It checks a
small, fixed set of moments: roughly a third and two-thirds of the way through each playing
period, plus every natural break (typically half-time). This is a deliberate, disclosed search
boundary — a practical way to keep planning tractable, not a claim about football doctrine. A
coach's own manually added or edited change is never restricted to these moments.

## How a batch of changes is decided

There is no fixed number of substitutions Matchboard always makes, and no fixed rhythm applied to
every match. At each candidate moment, Matchboard looks at which on-pitch outfield players are
genuinely "due" a change: they have played a meaningful stint (at least a few minutes, though a
natural break waives this) and are meaningfully ahead of a fair-share baseline for the match so
far. However many players meet that bar at once become one batch of changes together. A generated
plan for one squad might make one change every ten minutes; another might hold steady and make
several changes together at half-time. Neither pattern is treated as inherently better or riskier
— which one fits depends on the squad and the match.

## What decides who comes on

When a change is due, the bench candidate who replaces the outgoing player is chosen from:

- **role suitability** — see
  [Role suitability and position experience](/docs/how-matchboard-works/role-suitability-and-position-experience).
  A player with no support at all for the role is never excluded outright, but scores lowest;
- **fairness under-share** — how far behind a fair share of this match's minutes the candidate
  currently is, the same principle covered in
  [Fair playing opportunity](/docs/how-matchboard-works/fair-playing-opportunity);
- a small, capped bonus when the club has established evidence that a particular opponent
  tendency favours a specific tactical function, and a bench candidate fits it — for example,
  preserving a fast, pressing profile against an opponent known to build patiently from the back;
- a second small, capped bonus from position-context evidence where it exists (see
  [Evidence and learning](/docs/evidence-and-learning)).

Every one of these evidence-informed contributions is bounded and layered after role suitability
and fairness, never before them, and after every scoring step Matchboard checks structurally that
no previously-eligible candidate was silently excluded by an evidence signal — evidence can
reorder or nudge a choice, never remove someone from consideration.

## The goalkeeper is never part of this

Goalkeeper changes are never generated automatically. Who plays in goal, and for how long,
remains a decision the coach makes directly, exactly as in the lineup engine.

## Why this is not claimed to be globally optimal

The engine searches a bounded, practical set of decision points and builds the plan step by step,
committing to the best available batch at each point rather than exhaustively comparing every
possible whole-match sequence against every other. This is disclosed deliberately: Matchboard
does not claim its generated plan is the single best possible whole-match sequence, only that each
step is reasonable given the state the plan has reached by that point.

## Generating a lineup and rotation plan together

Building a starting lineup first and asking a rotation plan to work around whatever unfairness or
awkward structure that leaves behind is exactly what Matchboard avoids when a coach uses "Generate
lineup & rotation plan." Both stages read from the same picture of fairness, role suitability, and
evidence, computed once and shared — the starting eleven is chosen already aware of the same
context the rotation plan will use, not in ignorance of it. This is not the same as searching
every combination of starting lineup against every combination of rotation plan together; it means
neither stage is blind to what the other already knows.

## During the match

A planned change is a prompt during live reporting — apply it, delay it, or skip it against what
is actually happening. Applying it records the real position or player change on the actual match
timeline, at the real time it happened; a delayed change stays available to apply later; the
original plan is never silently rewritten to match reality. See
[Rotations](/docs/rotations) and
[Match state and the actual timeline](/docs/how-matchboard-works/match-state-and-actual-timeline)
for how the plan and the actual record stay distinct.

## Coach control

Every change a generated plan proposes can be edited, reordered, or removed exactly like one the
coach added directly. A generated plan is a starting point to react to, never a fixed script.
